## Talking points

- Tessa's three red-pen drafts are the thread. Each instruction was a wish about how the reply should feel.
- The specificity test: would two readers agree whether the reply followed it? If they could argue, the model can too.
- A never is a wall with a hole next to it. Pair it with an instead.
- Voice rules should be gradable with a highlighter; if one needs judgment, split it.
- A refusal is a behavior with parts, not a no. DR-2 shows all four: what, why from the record, what Tessa can do, the flag.
- Priority is where conflicts get decided once. Rule 2 rescues DR-2, rule 3 rescues "be concise."
- The new-hire test finds missing facts; delete-and-compare finds dead lines. Both are needed.

## Common misconceptions

- "Specific means long." Specific means checkable. "No exclamation marks" is four words and fully specific.
- "Negative instructions do not work." They work as edges. They fail when nothing says what fills the space they clear.
- "The refusal is a polite decline." It is a behavior: what, why, what Tessa can do, and a flag. A decline with no next step is a silent refusal in a nicer voice.
- "The model will figure out which rule matters more." It will, differently on each run. The priority block decides once.
- "Adding a rule can only help." Every line costs tokens and can contradict another line. Lines that change no output are cut.
